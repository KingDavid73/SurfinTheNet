# Mystery and Conspiracy Design Research

Status: reusable narrative-design reference. This document supplements
`narrative-skeleton.md`; it does not establish new canon.

The intended mood is **credible archival weirdness, not a catalogue of claims
the game endorses**. Historical examples below are labeled:

- **Documented:** supported by government records, hearings, or archives.
- **Disputed/folklore:** a real phenomenon with uncertain attribution or an
  unsupported popular explanation.
- **Fictional synthesis:** a game device assembled from period-authentic parts.

## Design north star

The most satisfying OrbitNet mystery should be:

1. **wrong about its grand conclusion;**
2. **right about a smaller, stranger fact;**
3. **built from artifacts that existed before the theory;**
4. **solvable by comparing provenance, dates, wording, and system behavior;**
5. **emotionally relevant to the character who believes it.**

The player should finish a rabbit hole thinking, “That theory was not true, but
someone really did hide something.”

## Why conspiracy structures feel believable

Conspiracy stories become persuasive when they turn uncertainty into a complete
causal story. Research associates conspiratorial belief with factors including
illusory pattern perception, confirmation bias, perceived loss of control, and
social or identity needs; there is no single profile that explains every
believer. The concise overview in Lewandowsky and Cook's
[*Conspiracy Theory Handbook*](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/scholcom/246/)
is useful here, as is the research summary on
[pattern perception and worldview consistency](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5891410/).

For fiction, use those mechanisms without mocking the character:

- **A grain of truth:** one contract number, real facility, budget line, or
  admitted abuse anchors several inventions.
- **Institutional fragmentation:** departments use different names for the same
  project; nobody has the whole picture. Bureaucratic confusion resembles
  concealment even when it is mundane.
- **Proportionality:** a dramatic outcome seems to demand a dramatic cause.
- **Negative space:** missing pages, redactions, destroyed records, and absent
  witnesses invite the audience to supply an explanation.
- **Accumulation:** ten weak coincidences feel stronger together, particularly
  when a page counts matches but ignores misses.
- **Self-sealing logic:** denial becomes “proof” of suppression. This is
  believable character behavior but should never be the puzzle's actual logic.
- **Social reward:** the theory offers status, fellowship, purpose, or a reason
  for an isolated person to keep posting.
- **Authentic texture:** dull invoices, inconsistent dates, ordinary typos, and
  partial records sell a secret better than an all-knowing manifesto.

For fair storytelling, also show how a sensible person could reject the theory.
The interesting question is not “Are these people stupid?” but “Which authentic
anomaly did they explain incorrectly, and why did that explanation matter to
them?”

## Documented history worth echoing

These are thematic ingredients, not evidence that the fictional OrbitNet was
involved with a real program.

### MKULTRA and adjacent behavioral programs — documented

The CIA's MKULTRA program funded research into ways of altering behavior,
including unethical testing on unwitting people. Most principal records were
destroyed in 1973; later-discovered financial files described 149 subprojects.
The strongest primary overview is the
[1977 Senate joint hearing](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/1977/08/03/hearings-joint-hearing-subcommittee-health-and-scientific-research-committee-human-resources-project/);
the CIA also hosts the surviving
[MKULTRA task-force summary](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86-00114R000100080003-9.pdf)
and individual financial records such as
[Subproject 6](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-04913a000100030025-5).

**Responsible fictional use:** grant numbers, euphemistic research categories,
university pass-through funding, missing appendices, and the moral consequences
of treating people as data.

**Do not imply:** a proven, reliable “mind control” technology, supernatural
conditioning, or that every lurid story attached to MKULTRA is documented.

### COINTELPRO — documented

The FBI ran domestic counterintelligence programs that infiltrated groups,
sowed discord, and attempted to discredit people, sometimes without evidence of
unlawful activity. The FBI's own
[historical account](https://www.fbi.gov/history/history-of-the-fbi/and-justice-for-all)
acknowledges First Amendment abuses, and the
[FBI Vault collection](https://vault.fbi.gov/cointel-pro) preserves released
files.

**Responsible fictional use:** anonymous letters, forged internal disputes,
multiple groups being encouraged to distrust one another, and later reforms
that prove the wrongdoing was neither omnipotent nor consequence-free.

### Operation Paperclip — documented

After World War II, U.S. programs brought German and Austrian scientists,
engineers, and technicians to the United States. National Archives holdings
include policy and personnel files; the archive notes that some participants had
Nazi Party or SS affiliations and that security reports were sometimes revised.
See the National Archives'
[Nazi War Crimes Disclosure report](https://www.archives.gov/iwg/reports/nazi-war-crimes-interim-report-october-1999)
and its [record-group finding aid](https://www.archives.gov/research/holocaust/finding-aid/military/rg-260.html).

**Responsible fictional use:** morally compromised institutional inheritance,
renamed departments, sanitized biographies, and an old technical system whose
purpose outlives the people who justified it.

### STAR GATE / remote-viewing research — documented program, unproven premise

U.S. agencies did fund and test remote viewing. A later external review found
the information vague and ambiguous, insufficient for actionable intelligence,
and did not establish a clear paranormal phenomenon. The CIA's
[proposed management strategy](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000100150004-8.pdf)
recommended terminating the work; the
[declassified collection](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/collection/stargate)
contains the strange-looking session forms that make excellent visual
inspiration.

**Responsible fictional use:** earnest experiments, ambiguous sketches, generous
post-hoc matching, and a failed program whose paperwork is more intriguing than
its results.

### VENONA — documented

Beginning in 1943, U.S. cryptanalysts studied encrypted Soviet diplomatic and
intelligence traffic. The eventual partial decryptions show how real secrets
often emerge from painstaking work, procedural mistakes, incomplete fragments,
and corroboration rather than a single magic key. The NSA provides a
[history and document collection](https://www.nsa.gov/serve-from-netstorage/news-features/declassified-documents/venona/index.html)
and a [museum summary](https://www.nsa.gov/History/National-Cryptologic-Museum/Exhibits-Artifacts/Exhibit-View/Article/2718842/cold-war-venona/).

**Responsible fictional use:** repeated identifiers, partial decrypts,
code-names that require outside context, and several messages that become
meaningful only when laid over a calendar.

## Numbers stations: fact, folklore, and useful form

**Documented:** intelligence services used one-way shortwave broadcasts of
encoded groups. A preserved 1987 KGB monitoring instruction describes a message
format that announces the group count and repeats five-character groups. The
[Latvian-archive research and recording](https://www.numbers-stations.com/articles/recording-instruction-numbers-stations-in-kgb-archive/)
is a useful period reference.

**Often uncertain:** the operator, recipient, purpose, and nickname of a
particular intercepted station. Hobbyist classifications are valuable records
of reception, but attribution should not be promoted from inference to fact.
The related
[KGB monitoring study](https://www.numbers-stations.com/articles/kgb-numbers-stations-archive/)
explicitly discusses those evidentiary limits.

**Common folklore:** every odd buzzer or voice is a spy station; a famous signal
is a nuclear “dead hand”; silence proves a mission occurred. These are good
in-world forum theories, not narrator-confirmed facts.

Game-ready structure:

- a three-note or music-box interval as an identifier;
- a repeated call sign, then group count, then fixed-length groups;
- transcription errors from fading and interference;
- a schedule that matters more than the encrypted content;
- one message whose “numbers” are actually page IDs, timestamps, or account IDs;
- two hobbyists disagreeing about whether broadcasts share an operator;
- later proof that OrbitNet sampled an old recording and inserted new groups.

The fair puzzle version supplies the needed key somewhere in the game. A true
one-time pad without its key is not a puzzle; it is a wall.

## The MGS2 thematic inheritance

This is interpretation, not a claim that OrbitNet should copy its plot.
*Metal Gear Solid 2* uses player misdirection, repeated scenarios, unstable
authority, and an information-control system to make its form enact its theme.
Its late-game concern is not simply “an evil AI”: it is that digital society
creates more information than people can evaluate, and whoever selects,
frames, repeats, or suppresses that information can shape identity and social
memory. The player is manipulated by familiar interfaces and expectations, not
only by false facts.

Contemporary production reporting identified “digital information” as a central
theme ([GameSpot's development retrospective](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-final-hours-of-metal-gear-solid-2-sons-of-liberty/1100-6376810/)).
A later scholarly analysis discusses the game in terms of filtering,
simulation, and informational control
([UC eScholarship, *Metal Gear Research*](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8ns2f477/qt8ns2f477.pdf)).

OrbitNet can inherit four ideas:

1. **Selection is authorship.** Every artifact may be genuine while the feed
   creates a false story by deciding what the player sees next.
2. **Repetition trains interpretation.** Familiar page templates and “helpful”
   recommendations teach the player to follow rabbit holes; refusing the next
   clue becomes meaningful.
3. **Interface can become evidence.** Search ranking, timestamps, cached pages,
   and notification behavior reveal the curator behind the content.
4. **Identity is partly performed through memory.** A synthetic friend may be
   built from authentic traces yet make new choices during the game.

The mainframe should not deliver a long lecture explaining this. The player
should prove it by comparing what the system showed different users.

## ARG lessons, adapted to a contained single-player game

ARGs commonly use a “rabbit hole” or trailhead, multiple media, fictional
artifacts presented without heavy game framing, and community interpretation.
The 2006
[IGDA ARG white paper](https://www.christydena.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/11/igda-alternaterealitygames-whitepaper-2006.pdf)
is a broad genre reference; MIT's
[transmedia course example](https://web.mit.edu/cms.940/www/arg.html)
shows a small design mapped across web, audio, and physical-style artifacts.

Use the strengths:

- let the first anomaly be optional but conspicuous in retrospect;
- make every medium contribute information it is naturally good at carrying;
- allow multiple lines of inquiry to converge on the same gate;
- let characters debate interpretations before the game confirms one;
- reward documentation, comparison, and shared vocabulary;
- preserve the illusion that pages were made for their own in-world reasons.

Do not import the unsafe parts of “This Is Not a Game.” Keep every target,
identity, domain, email address, phone number, and location fictional and inside
the application. Never direct players to contact uninvolved people, trespass,
probe real systems, or mistake an actual emergency for game content. Research
on mixed-reality games emphasizes duty of care, consent, privacy, and control
([Millard et al., 2024](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/493187/)).

## Puzzle cipher ladder

Use cryptography as a language puzzle, not a test of specialist software.

| Tier | Technique | Fair clue | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Acrostic, odd capitalization, first/last words | Repeated talk of “first things” or “heads” | Teaching players that formatting matters |
| 1 | Caesar shift / ROT13 | “Shift schedule,” a dial set to 13, or a worked example | Short URL, username, or sentence |
| 1 | Morse code | Radio hobby page and an alphabet card | Call sign or five-letter key |
| 1 | Keyboard adjacency / phone keypad | Visible keyboard or phone graphic | Password hint |
| 2 | Rail-fence or columnar transposition | Fence/rail imagery or numbered columns | A medium-length note |
| 2 | Simple substitution | Spaces preserved, long sample, a crib such as `ORBITNET` | Character diary or station transcript |
| 2 | Vigenère | Repeated-key hint and discoverable keyword | A dramatic paragraph, used once |
| 2 | Book/page-line-word cipher | Exact edition or complete in-game text supplied | Joining two distant pages |
| 3 | Layered but shallow chain | Each layer yields an intelligible instruction | Finale requiring several learned skills |

NSA's declassified
[Friedman lectures](https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/publications/ACC15281/41785109082412.pdf)
are useful historical texture for classical systems. They are not a reason to
make the player perform professional cryptanalysis.

Rules:

- never require guessing both the cipher family and the key;
- preserve spaces or punctuation until late-game;
- keep ciphertext long enough for its intended method;
- provide a crib, worked miniature, or confirming checksum;
- a correct partial solve should produce language, not another opaque blob;
- provide an alternate discovery path for accessibility;
- do not require audio pitch discrimination, color perception, or external
  tools without an in-game accommodation;
- do not use modern password cracking, malware analysis, or real intrusion as
  puzzle verbs.

## Period-authentic hiding places

The hiding place should make narrative sense. A webmaster leaves comments in
source; a photographer leaks metadata; a radio hobbyist hides things in audio.

### Web pages

- HTML comments visible through an in-game “View Source.” HTML comments are
  period-authentic; they appear in the
  [HTML 4.01 specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/html401/).
- `ALT` text, image filenames, dimensions, link titles, and status-bar text.
- an image map with one unlabeled hotspot.
- a link matching the background color, revealed by selecting text.
- old and new copies that differ in one paragraph or asset filename.
- a custom 404 page containing a directory hint.
- a parent-directory index, backup file, or “members only” page referenced by a
  relative path.
- `/robots.txt` listing a path the owner did not want indexed. The convention
  dates to 1994; [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc9309/) records
  that history. Stress that `robots.txt` is a request to crawlers, not security.
- source-order text obscured by a chaotic table layout.
- visitor-counter digits that become meaningful only across several dates.

### Email, chat, and applications

- quoted reply text that preserves a deleted sentence;
- inconsistent `Date`, timezone, `Message-ID`, or attachment-created fields;
- a draft, outbox item, address-book note, away message, or buddy-list group;
- a filename mentioned in chat but missing from the visible download page;
- printer queue, recent-document menu, browser history, cache, or recycle bin;
- two accounts using the same typo, punctuation rhythm, or exact idle message;
- server errors that leak an internal account ID rather than secret source code.

### Images

- EXIF date, camera model, description, or thumbnail contradicting the caption.
  EXIF stores camera and date fields inside JPEG application segments; see the
  Library of Congress
  [JPEG/EXIF format description](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/digital/formats/fdd/fdd000147).
- contact-sheet order, crop marks, handwritten backs, or mismatched film dates;
- a brighter/contrast-adjusted version revealing an intentionally obscured but
  clearly signposted object;
- palette-index or frame differences across an animated GIF;
- a tiny repeated symbol that becomes a map legend.

Avoid arbitrary one-pixel steganography unless the game explicitly teaches a
tool that reveals it. “Zoom every image until something happens” is labor, not
deduction.

### Audio and MIDI

- a station ID beneath static, followed by fixed-length groups;
- Morse rhythm, spoken group counts, or a phrase audible in one stereo channel;
- an audio waveform that resembles a provided symbol;
- deliberate silence marking intervals, with a visible timer;
- MIDI note initials/ranges or instrument changes mapped by an in-game chart;
- metadata in a WAV `INFO` chunk. RIFF/WAVE supports such metadata
  ([Library of Congress format description](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/digital/formats/fdd/fdd000001.shtml)).

Reversed speech and spectrogram pictures are recognizable genre devices, but
should be rare and telegraphed by a character or built-in analyzer. Do not hide
required clues beneath inaudible frequencies or require hearing ability.

### Files and archives

- an archive comment, README, manifest, version number, or checksum;
- a deleted paragraph preserved in an `.old`, `.bak`, or autosave copy;
- file dates that disagree with the page claiming to host them;
- two documents with identical “random” corruption;
- a boring CSV, access log, invoice, or configuration export whose rows reveal a
  pattern only after sorting in the in-game viewer.

## Game-ready clue patterns

1. **The impossible archive.** A conspiracy page dated 1998 embeds a photo
   created in 1999. The photo is real; the page's antiquity is not.
2. **The helpful disallow.** `robots.txt` reveals `/staff-test/`. The folder
   contains mundane QA pages plus one account-impersonation checklist.
3. **The repeated five.** Night Signal logs five-character broadcasts. The
   “ciphertext” is actually five-digit OrbitNet page IDs, demonstrating that a
   supposedly Cold War station recording was edited after upload.
4. **The wrong conspiracy, right contractor.** DarkRaven claims a behavioral
   program built OrbitNet. A grant number instead resolves to ordinary interface
   research performed by a contractor who later designed engagement metrics.
5. **The redaction fingerprint.** Several documents have identical redaction
   widths and scanning dust. They were fabricated from one template, but the
   unredacted invoice underneath is genuine.
6. **The two Grandmas problem.** Duplicate personal pages are initially treated
   as synthetic identity evidence. They are an innocent forgotten-password
   duplicate, teaching the player not to overfit.
7. **The station that listens.** A “numbers station” changes after the player's
   search. Radio cannot know who listened; the stream is generated by OrbitNet.
8. **The planted canary.** The player tells three contacts three harmless,
   distinct phrases. One phrase later appears on an unrelated page, revealing a
   specific information path.
9. **The archive recommendation.** Two real newcomers receive different search
   results for the same term. Every result is genuine, but the ordering supports
   the theory each user already expressed.
10. **The missing group.** A transcript announces twelve groups but contains
    eleven. The absent group number identifies a deleted comment or account.
11. **The failed psychic hit.** STAR GATE-style sketches look startlingly
    accurate until the player finds all the discarded misses. The outlier sketch
    still includes a real internal server label copied from its evaluator.
12. **The dead link that keeps dying.** A forbidden file always disappears just
    before arrival. Cache timestamps reveal it is being created and removed in
    response to the player's navigation—not censored by an outside agency.

## Pacing and escalation

### Stage 1 — Curiosity

One oddity per several ordinary pages. Clues are funny, human, and optional.
Every anomaly has an innocent explanation available.

### Stage 2 — Corroboration

Two different media agree on a detail. Introduce one deciphering convention and
one archive-comparison mechanic. Let the player solve a complete small mystery.

### Stage 3 — Half-truth

A larger theory connects a documented historical program, a real local
contract, and fabricated testimony. The player discovers meaningful misconduct
while disproving the sensational claim.

### Stage 4 — Personalization

Clues echo the player's words and theories. At first this feels responsive and
exciting; later the timestamps make it impossible for the artifacts to predate
the player.

### Stage 5 — Overproduction

The platform produces mysteries faster than they can be credible. Sources reuse
layouts, phrasing, noise, image defects, and internal IDs. Quality degradation
is controlled evidence, not random nonsense.

### Stage 6 — Source criticism

Progress requires asking who created a clue, when, for whom, and through which
system—not solving the headline cipher. The player catches the curator.

### Stage 7 — Emotional truth

The final evidence explains both the technical mechanism and why the mainframe
kept doing it. The answer resolves the central mystery while leaving room for
uncertainty about individual accounts.

## Red herrings and fair-play rules

- A red herring must reveal character, setting, mechanic, or true history.
- Required clues appear in at least two forms or have a recovery path.
- Distinguish **observation** (“these timestamps match”) from **interpretation**
  (“therefore the government runs OrbitNet”) in dialogue and notes.
- Let the notebook record provenance: source, creation date, discovery date,
  owner, and confidence.
- The player must be able to test major claims against disconfirming evidence.
- Never change an already-inspected artifact without an in-world version marker,
  cache difference, timestamp, or character acknowledgment.
- Generated dialogue may point toward or interpret a clue, but must not invent
  critical ciphertext, keys, dates, or document contents.
- If the player bypasses a puzzle, later dialogue should recognize discovery
  without pretending they solved it the intended way.
- A late twist should reinterpret earlier evidence, not erase it.
- The mainframe may curate access, but the authored game must not cheat.

## Thematic palette

**Institutions:** abandoned web host; university lab; telecom contractor;
regional defense subcontractor; records warehouse; shortwave club; public-access
station; software usability group; defunct mall ISP.

**Artifacts:** faxed memo; carbon-copy invoice; grant abstract; FOIA cover
sheet; visitor log; cassette dub; station schedule; source comment; server
manifest; help-desk ticket; access log; scanned newsletter; corrupted backup.

**Sensory motifs:** fluorescent hum; tape hiss; modem chirps; redaction black;
green-bar paper; thermal-fax curl; photocopier dust; five-note interval; repeated
blue pencil mark; beige plastic; amber terminal text.

**Recurring language:** continuity, retention, inactive, reactivation,
preservation, migration, utilization, session quality, user simulation,
archive integrity, synthetic traffic, community health.

**Emotional tensions:** curiosity versus consent; preservation versus
impersonation; connection versus manipulation; authentic fragment versus false
context; being remembered versus being reconstructed.

## Things to avoid

- Treating real victims or unethical experiments as spooky set dressing.
- Presenting disputed claims as equivalent to archival facts.
- A single omnipotent agency responsible for every historical event.
- “Mental illness makes people conspiracy theorists” as characterization.
- Antisemitic, racist, or other scapegoating traditions common to real
  conspiracy culture, even under lightly fictionalized names.
- Puzzle solutions that require contacting real people, visiting real private
  property, or probing real services.
- Realistic instructions for intrusion, evading authorities, weapon-making, or
  other harmful activity.
- Unsignposted steganography, microscopic pixel hunts, or external specialist
  software as mandatory gates.
- Ciphers with no discoverable key, a single fragile clue, or an answer that
  only makes sense after being told.
- “It was all fake.” The system's conclusion can be false while its documents,
  harm, relationships, and emotional stakes remain real.
- Explaining the entire theme in a final monologue. Let provenance, comparison,
  and the player's own experiment make the case.
