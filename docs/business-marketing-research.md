# Existing business campaign research

This pass expands the three original search-only businesses without changing
their established owners or homepage URLs. Each company uses a different
late-1990s commercial-web language and a company-specific image sheet.

## Byte Barn: local expertise in a national-catalog market

Byte Barn borrows the dense comparison tables, explicit specifications, price
bursts, product cutouts, and clean navy/red/yellow structure of 1999 computer
manufacturers and mail-order circulars. The difference is its local position:
plain advice, known components, house calls, and no hold music.

References:

- [Web Design Museum: Dell in 1999](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/dell-in-1999)
- [Web Design Museum: Hewlett Packard in 1999](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/hewlett-packard-in-1999)
- [Internet Archive: Dell Home Systems catalog, January 1999](https://archive.org/details/dell-home-systems-catalog-1999-01)
- [Web Design Museum: Macromedia Online Store in 1999](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/macromedia-online-store-in-1999)

Campaign sheet medium: slightly over-sharpened product photography from a
scanned Sunday computer circular.

## Cosmic Crust: local coupon flyer meets novelty restaurant

Cosmic Crust keeps the direct value language of late-1990s chain restaurant
sites and print coupons: huge food, meal bundles, printable offers, a telephone
number, delivery limits, and a dense menu. Its neighborhood identity comes from
the homemade space theme, two arcade cabinets, and Toni's visible uncertainty
about online ordering.

References:

- [Web Design Museum: Subway in 1999](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/subway-in-1999)
- [Web Design Museum: Pizza Hut in 1996](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/pizza-hut-1996)
- [Food & Wine: the early PizzaNet ordering site](https://www.foodandwine.com/pizza-net-delivery-history-11771677)

Campaign sheet medium: saturated flash-lit food photography and kitschy
airbrushed outer-space collage from a local direct-mail flyer.

## Paws & Claws: owner-made scrapbook, not dot-com warehouse

Paws & Claws deliberately avoids the national one-stop-shop or venture-funded
pet e-commerce voice. The page feels maintained by Bev: instant snapshots,
paper scraps, practical aquarium advice, a store cat, and a careful adoption
process. Product categories remain visible, but care and local relationships
are the primary pitch.

References:

- [SFGate: Pets.com homepage and retrospective](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/pets-dot-com-sock-puppet-look-back-18205346.php)
- [Wired: “A Dog-Eat-Dotcom World” (2000)](https://www.wired.com/2000/05/a-dog-eat-dotcom-world)
- [Web Design Museum: 1999 gallery](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/gallery/year-1999)

Campaign sheet medium: faded amateur 35mm snapshots and mail-order catalog
clippings assembled as an owner-made scrapbook.

## Production rule

The built-in image-generation path produces one consistent 3-by-2 sheet per
company on a removable chroma-key background. The sheets are converted to alpha,
cropped into six local assets, and committed alongside the page. Art direction
is selected per fictional business; batching is only an efficiency technique,
not a shared style.
