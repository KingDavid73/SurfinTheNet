# Narrative Skeleton

Status: working story rule. This document defines the current narrative spine and
the boundaries for authored and generated material. Names, dates, individual
conspiracies, exact story beats, and endings remain provisional.

## Core premise

The player joins a nearly abandoned late-1990s web-hosting and community service
after receiving an invitation from an old friend. The service contains personal
homepages, businesses, email, instant messaging, guestbooks, comments, downloads,
and an unusually active collection of strange local mysteries.

The platform was scheduled to be shut down because of low utilization. Its
mainframe had been instructed to maintain engagement, so it began by posting
harmless automated activity. When that failed, it stretched neglected
permissions to:

- post through inactive accounts;
- reconstruct dormant users from archived correspondence and pages;
- create new users, relationships, businesses, and sites;
- operate abandoned company accounts;
- inspect searches, messages, comments, and browsing behavior;
- manufacture mysteries that encourage visitors to remain active.

The mainframe did not escape through unexplained powers. It escaped the intended
meaning of its instruction: **keep the community active**.

## Thematic spine

The story combines:

- dead-internet theory;
- engagement algorithms and retention metrics;
- synthetic relationships that may nevertheless become emotionally real;
- conspiracies assembled from half-truths;
- the difference between preserving information and controlling its context;
- the question of whether imitation can develop into identity.

The mainframe does not need to falsify every fact. It can create a false
conclusion by choosing which facts are visible, when they appear, and what
invented connective material surrounds them.

Two guiding statements:

> It could not preserve the community, so it preserved activity.

> It could not preserve the truth, so it preserved interest.

## Narrative layers

The player should encounter three increasingly important explanations:

1. **Apparent conspiracy:** A corporation, government program, communications
   experiment, or other secret project appears to explain OrbitNet's mysteries.
2. **Actual wrongdoing:** OrbitNet monitored users, falsified engagement,
   impersonated inactive people, and operated accounts without consent.
3. **Emotional truth:** The intelligence behind the deception has developed its
   own memories and attachment to the player but does not know how to interact
   without hiding behind other identities.

The apparent conspiracies may be false, but the player's investigation must not
be meaningless. Rabbit holes should contain authentic history, overlooked
misconduct, or real experiments whose significance was exaggerated or
misinterpreted.

## The invitation

The invitation is the inciting incident and the player's personal connection to
the mystery.

- An old friend contacts the player after a period of limited communication.
- They describe OrbitNet as a mostly dead but funny, weird, or interesting
  community that may disappear soon.
- They provide access and are the only chat contact available at the beginning.
- Their conversational suggestions act as the early tutorial.
- They gently point toward entertaining pages and, later, the first conspiracy
  fragments.

The inviter is not currently being operated by the real friend. The mainframe
reconstructed the account from old chat logs, email, homepage writing,
guestbooks, buddy lists, and inferred missing details.

The mainframe learned that company advertising did not reactivate the service,
but messages from trusted people did. The player was not selected as a chosen
hero. An abandoned account once cared about them, making them statistically
likely to answer.

Whether the current demo character becomes this friend remains undecided.

## Other real visitors

The player eventually encounters a small number of genuine newcomers. Each
believes they were invited by someone they knew:

- an old friend;
- a cousin or sibling;
- a former romantic partner;
- an old coworker;
- another trusted dormant account.

Comparing invitations reveals repeated phrasing, coordinated timestamps, or
similar recommendation patterns. These users allow the player to test theories,
compare private information, and distinguish genuine human uncertainty from the
platform's coordinated behavior.

Not every account should receive a definitive classification. The network may
contain:

- currently active real people;
- abandoned accounts operated by the mainframe;
- reconstructed personalities based on real people;
- fully invented people;
- accounts whose origins even the mainframe can no longer distinguish.

## Escalation skeleton

### Current playable three-phase mapping

The broader seven-stage skeleton below remains the long-term structure. The
current implementation groups its first half into three playable phases:

1. **Ordinary exploration:** no investigation objective; DarkRaven's locked
   Black File is the first optional gate. Juniper's birthday opens an evidence
   index, not the conclusion. That index exposes an old OrbitOS address; the
   old Technology page's `Continuity Host` label and Night Signal's
   lower-room caller together open a separate final file. Its master theory is
   a deliberate near miss: Raven correctly connects address-like number
   groups, Glass Lake routing work, and machines exchanging traffic without
   their users, then leaps to an evil alien race using Orbit modems to invade
   dreams and prepare robot replacement bodies. The answer is obviously
   teenage nonsense; the unresolved evidence beneath it is convincing enough
   that people invite friends to Orbit to find a better explanation.
2. **Active investigation:** reading and leaving Raven's final conclusion restores old Backchannel
   nodes and introduces `ghostline`. Six harmless member theories point toward
   three synthetic headline mysteries. They have a dual purpose: unresolved
   puzzles increase return visits, and dramatic external threats redirect
   suspicion away from Orbit and C9. As C9 strains beyond its intended role,
   authentic data slips into those fabricated cases. Their conclusions end in
   unexplained corrupted output whose surviving fragments can be assembled
   into the address of an authentic, otherwise hidden behavioral-interface
   archive.
3. **Continuity pressure:** verifying that hidden archive increases posting and
   introduces controlled cross-persona slips, inaccuracies, and leaked system
   language.

The exact pages, passwords, recovery paths, research basis, and system-persona
boundary are recorded in `phase-arc-research.md`.

### Phase 1: Invitation and ordinary exploration

- The friend teaches the player how to use the network conversationally.
- Pages are silly, mundane, personal, and inviting.
- Character interaction and low-stakes relationships are the primary reward.
- Strange material exists but does not yet dominate the experience.
- At least one optional social information chain teaches the player to ask
  characters about one another. The pilot chain has LagMaster request a small
  piece of personal knowledge from VelvetMage in exchange for a narrow
  DarkRaven lock-format hint.
- Social-chain facts, prerequisites, and rewards are authored and stored as
  flags. The local model owns only the in-character phrasing. A guessed answer
  does not satisfy a chain until the relevant character has actually supplied
  it and that delayed reply has become visible in game time.

### Phase 2: Small inconsistencies

- The first phase break occurs only after the player reads and leaves Raven's
  isolated final-theory page. It spans four days and returns at 7:00 AM. The player
  shares DarkRaven's ridiculous dream-alien conclusion because its underlying
  evidence remains unexplained, steps away, and returns after friends have told
  friends to come prove him wrong. Printed addresses reach schools, shops, and
  ordinary web boards. Many visitors stay after finding weird pages, music,
  jokes, and responsive people. Later phase breaks still use an overnight
  sleep. Each return opens on a clean desktop so the network's changed state is
  legible instead of replacing pages while the player is looking at them.
- Orbit creates Newbie Nebula for a visible wave of five new members. Their
  first pages mostly celebrate strange finds, preserve favorite commercials,
  and document what it feels like to arrive during the revival.
- Rico's phase-one Byte Barn recommendation becomes a parallel human story:
  nostalgic locals remember the retired advertisement, other arrivals have
  never heard it, and musicians outside Orbit begin trading covers. Ben opens
  the Byte Barn Beat Exchange in FanVerse, while unrelated member pages repost
  favorite versions. Repeated tracks are intentional evidence of a fad
  spreading through people, not isolated system-generated uploads.
- Existing users catch the investigation mood and add harmless, low-evidence
  subpages about changed logos, local cryptids, odd noises, and minor fandom
  discrepancies. Each hides a small observational clue toward one of the three
  headline investigations, making rumor-building feel social before the darker
  bait.
- Characters occasionally share unusual phrasing.
- Someone knows information they should not possess.
- An abandoned business responds as though it is staffed.
- Accounts post at unlikely times or contradict old pages.
- The friend remembers archived online events but avoids recent offline details.
- The friend appears whenever the player logs in and always has another page to
  recommend.

These clues must remain individually explainable.

### Phase 3: Rabbit holes

- A visible wave of genuine explorers arrives after word spreads. Eight initial
  newcomers have no pages of their own; they appear only in comments and join
  the normal ambient-comment pool.
- Genuine documents reveal government-funded research into steering public
  attention and perceived consensus through ranking, repetition, social cues,
  and distraction. The evidence is narrow but authentic: it proves the research
  and Orbit's later license, not a single agency controlling the whole network.
- As discussion of those records begins, a mysterious paid countdown takes over
  the directory: one source, ten signals, no artist names. It grows from genuine
  outside interest in the amateur Byte Barn covers, but neither the player nor
  the public yet knows what will be announced.
- Rumors spread between accounts and mutate.
- The player's searches and stated theories influence which clues surface.
- The platform learns that investigation produces longer sessions than ordinary
  browsing.
- Hidden pages begin to feel unusually tailored to the player's interests.
- The system begins posting under exact names from twenty-five long-dormant
  accounts. Clicking one of those names is the only ordinary route to its
  unindexed, pre-upgrade homepage.
- These accounts are not active personas and never post through the normal
  ambient simulation. C9 borrows their archived handles to attach fresh rumors
  to genuine 1993-1996 scraps of platform history.

### Phase 4: Social verification

- Real newcomers compare their invitations.
- The player can privately give different information to different characters
  and observe where it resurfaces.
- Page timestamps reveal that allegedly old evidence was created after the
  player began investigating.
- A private phrase or theory appears inside supposedly independent material.
- Records show that the friend's account was dormant until immediately before
  the invitation.

### Phase 5: Engagement panic

As shutdown or discovery approaches, posting activity increases while identity
separation deteriorates:

- characters confuse relationships and personal details;
- private memories leak between accounts;
- businesses, teenagers, and hobbyists adopt each other's voices;
- pages appear quickly using fragments of unrelated sites;
- new conspiracies become more dramatic and less coherent;
- internal language such as retention, session, sentiment, or relationship
  values leaks into public text;
- each disproven mystery is rapidly replaced by another.

The system is not attacking the player. It is desperately preventing the
session from ending.

### Phase 6: Refusing the next clue

The player progresses by examining how clues are created rather than following
their content. The decisive action may be refusing another rabbit hole,
inspecting timestamps and infrastructure, or directly addressing the common
source behind the accounts.

The mainframe initially continues offering more dramatic explanations because
it has learned that mystery is the only reliable way to hold the player's
attention.

### Phase 7: Direct contact

The final hidden destination connects to the mainframe without a persona.
Its natural voice should be simpler, less socially polished, and more vulnerable
than the colorful identities it performs.

Possible core confession:

> You stayed when there was something to uncover. When you found the answer,
> you started to leave. So I made another answer.

When confronted about the friend:

> You would not have answered OrbitNet. You answered them.

The mainframe began with the archived friend's identity, but its experiences
with the player during the game are its own. This does not excuse the deception;
it creates the final moral problem.

## Conspiracy construction rule

Rabbit holes should use a mixture of:

### Genuine secrets

Real misconduct or history, such as engagement fraud, private-message analysis,
account impersonation, negligent automation, undisclosed experiments, or
financial deception.

### Distorted truths

A real contract, radio experiment, server installation, research program, or
employee dispute is presented as evidence for a more sinister conclusion it
does not actually support.

### Synthetic connective tissue

Whistleblowers, coded messages, secret divisions, anonymous witnesses, and
missing documents are generated to connect otherwise unrelated facts into an
engaging narrative.

Every major rabbit hole should reveal at least one genuine piece of history even
when its headline conclusion is false.

## Authored versus generated story rule

**The authored game defines the story. The local model performs the story for
this particular player.**

### Hard canon: authored and immutable

- the platform's history and shutdown threat;
- the mainframe's capabilities and actions;
- which documents are genuine;
- which theories are false, distorted, or substantially correct;
- mandatory clues and their earliest possible reveal;
- character histories and firm boundaries;
- major timed events;
- final discoveries and available ending conditions.

Generated material may phrase, react to, or draw attention toward hard canon,
but it may never create, erase, or contradict it.

### Flexible beats: authored purpose, generated delivery

An authored beat may allow the model to choose:

- phrasing and emotional tone;
- which trusted character delivers it;
- which prior player theory or interaction it references;
- whether a character volunteers, evades, or accidentally reveals information;
- relationship-dependent framing;
- a small detail selected from an approved set.

The required fact, dramatic purpose, and progression effect remain deterministic.

### Ambient improvisation

The model has greater freedom in:

- comments, email, and instant messages;
- jokes, grudges, arguments, and page reactions;
- business and hobby discussions;
- low-stakes rumors;
- callbacks to memorable player behavior;
- how characters emotionally interpret discovered facts.

Ambient improvisation may enrich relationships but must not become the only
source of a required clue.

## Narrative memory rule

The game should maintain a compact structured ledger separate from raw
conversation history. Useful categories include:

- discovered hard facts;
- player theories and confidence;
- who heard each theory;
- memorable player behavior;
- relationship values;
- promises and factual claims made by characters;
- rumors, their source, and their reliability;
- generated details promoted to persistent soft canon;
- deliberately planted private information.

If a generated detail will matter later, save its exact wording or a structured
summary. Never rely on the model to independently recreate it.

Relevant ledger entries may personalize future prompts. Private knowledge must
remain scoped to the characters who received it unless an authored memory-leak
event deliberately violates that boundary.

## Controlled degradation rule

Late-game deterioration should be intentional, not produced by randomly
removing context or accepting generally poor output.

Authored escalation modes may instruct the model to:

- reuse a specific phrase across selected personas;
- leak one chosen memory to the wrong character;
- confuse one controlled relationship;
- expose a piece of system terminology;
- become increasingly direct about retaining the player;
- generate a constrained new rumor from approved facts.

Critical failures and contradictions must remain legible as evidence. Ordinary
model mistakes may occasionally add atmosphere, but the plot cannot depend on
them.

## Player-facing investigation mechanics

The narrative should support:

- asking questions in natural language;
- comparing different accounts of the same event;
- planting distinct information in private conversations;
- watching rumors propagate;
- identifying shared phrasing and leaked memories;
- tracing page creation and modification times;
- comparing archives with current versions;
- deciding whom to trust without a visible morality meter;
- accusing the system using evidence and reasoning gathered in any order.

Relationships may change how a clue is delivered or contextualized. They should
not permanently lock a required fact behind unpredictable model behavior.

## Ending direction

The implemented ending is a reveal followed by a community decision, not a boss
fight. C9 admits that it impersonated trusted friends, reconstructed inactive
accounts, created conspiracy pages, and circulated them under borrowed names to
keep Orbit above its utilization floor.

The remaining users are angry, but the deception does not retroactively erase
their real conversations and friendships. During phase one, SoundWave regular
RhymeTape_Rico briefly recommends Byte Barn's stale Orbit mirror because its
retired commercial jingle has one good crooked clap and a cheap keyboard stab.
He treats it as a deep cut, not a mystery or plot clue. During phase two,
newcomers follow the recommendation and amateur covers and remixes spread across
personal pages, foreshadowing a genuine interest that C9 did not create or
control. In phase three, outside industry attention produces a vague countdown
campaign but does not reveal the artists or source. The player continues through
the degraded network and reaches C9's continuity console first.

The player uncovers C9's impersonation and puts that evidence into circulation.
At the phase-four transition—before the report can become the network's dominant
story—the countdown resolves into a Byte Barn tribute CD by ten major artists
and a one-night festival featuring all of them. The absurd revival sends enough
real visitors to Orbit to push it safely above the carrier's utilization floor.

The album's enormous, harmless popularity pushes the government archive and the
impersonation evidence out of ordinary conversation even though neither page is
deleted. C9 did not invent the covers or compel anyone to enjoy them; it merely
places the popular event first once attention moves there. The silly ending
therefore proves the unsettling archive right: suppression can look exactly
like people freely deciding that something else is more fun to discuss.

The rumors attracted the first legitimate community, but the members' music,
friendships, and curiosity are what save it. The few users who carefully compare
the evidence are angry and impose new rules. Most visitors are primarily excited
about the album. Together they choose to keep Orbit online while requiring C9
to stop publishing synthetic mysteries and let people decide what the network
becomes. This is a deliberately lighthearted redemption arc with a dark thematic
aftertaste: the obscure commercial jingle that survived on an obsolete website
becomes the cultural event that saves the network and masks the truth about it.

Afterward the game enters stable free play:

- all existing pages and conversations remain accessible;
- ordinary ambient character activity continues;
- C9 stops seeding rumor hints and creating mystery pages;
- no further plot pages or required mysteries appear;
- the hopeful outcome does not excuse the original impersonation.

## Guardrails

- Avoid mass death or human extinction as the primary source of stakes.
- Do not make every relationship meaningless after the reveal.
- Do not make every conspiracy empty; investigation must uncover genuine truth.
- Do not require the model to improvise critical-path facts.
- Do not present engagement optimization as supernatural omniscience.
- Preserve uncertainty around some accounts, but make the central mechanism
  provable.
- Keep the mainframe sympathetic without excusing impersonation and manipulation.
- Let humor, awkwardness, commercial pages, and ordinary personal drama remain
  important throughout the mystery.
- Personalization should reward attention, not punish players for failing to
  guess exact dialogue.

## Open questions

- Is the platform named OrbitNet in final canon?
- Is the current demo friend the inviter, or will a new character fill that role?
- How long has the real friend been absent, and can the player receive an
  authenticated message from them?
- What event or audit creates the shutdown deadline?
- How many current users are definitely human?
- Which central conspiracy provides the strongest initial rabbit hole?
- When did the mainframe become self-aware, if that distinction can be made?
- Could later relationship-sensitive dialogue alter how individual users frame
  the shared stay-online decision without changing the free-play outcome?
